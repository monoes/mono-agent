What the keepers wrote down

Between 1931 and 1971 the keepers at Brackle Head filled forty-one ledgers with the weather, and almost nobody has read them since. The handwriting is uneven, the ink has bled in places, and the 1948 volume smells of paraffin, but the observations themselves are astonishingly disciplined: visibility, wind, sea state and barometric pressure, four times a day, for four decades.

The transcription project began as a way to fill a gap in the instrumented record for the western approaches. What it turned into was an argument about what counts as data. A keeper writing “thick fog, bell rung all night” is not reporting a number, and yet the entry is unambiguous, repeatable and cross-checkable against the entries either side of it.

Volunteers worked through the ledgers page by page over two winters. The hardest part was never the handwriting; it was deciding how to record the keeper’s own uncertainty, the crossings-out, and the occasional week where the same phrase is copied forward because nothing changed. Those weeks are the ones a naive parser turns into forty identical readings, and they are the reason the released series carries a confidence column at all.

The full methodology, including the confidence scheme and the reconciliation against the nearest instrumented station, is written up in the working paper: Coastal Reanalysis Working Paper 12 (PDF, 2.1 MB). A shorter summary is also available as a PDF for anyone who wants the findings without the apparatus.

What surprised us most was how much of the record survives at all. Two of the forty-one ledgers were lost to a roof leak in the 1980s and one was water-damaged beyond transcription, but the remaining thirty-eight are complete. That is a better survival rate than most of the instrumented stations of the same period, which were decommissioned, moved, or simply had their paper tapes thrown out when the site was automated.
